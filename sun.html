Where the spot is

The sun's position depends on latitude, longitude and the date, and on nothing else. GPS is far better than it needs to be — a hundred metres would not move the answer by a minute.

What the sun does here

What the compass is lying by

This is the whole feasibility question. A phone's magnetometer is good to somewhere between five and fifteen degrees, and worse beside a truck or a wire fence — and that error, not the astronomy, is what decides whether any of the rest is worth trusting. So measure it rather than hope.

Sight along a shadow. A shadow's bearing is the sun's bearing plus exactly 180°, and this page knows the sun's bearing to a tenth of a degree. Stand a post — or use your own shadow — point the phone straight down its length, and hold the button. What comes back is what your compass is out by. No looking at the sun required.

Magnetic declination

Android reports its heading from magnetic north, which is several degrees off true north nearly everywhere. iOS corrects this itself and this field is then left alone. Look yours up once on NOAA's calculator; the shadow sight above absorbs it either way, so it is belt over braces.

What was measured

The skyline

Put the crosshair on the top of whatever would get between this spot and the sun — a treeline, a barn, a ridge — and mark it. Twenty or thirty marks around the sun's own arc is a survey. Only the angle matters, never the height or the distance, so nothing has to be measured or paced out.
